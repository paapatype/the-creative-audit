How to write a design brief that gets you what you actually wanted

By SankalpThe Creative Audit

If you've ever received design work that was technically what you asked for and somehow nothing like what you wanted, I want you to know something first: it probably wasn't your designer's fault, and it almost certainly wasn't yours.

Nobody teaches founders how to brief. You're expected to speak a language fluently that you were never taught — and then blamed, quietly, when the translation fails. Here's the uncomfortable truth from the other side of the table: most rebrands don't fail at the logo. They fail at the brief. A designer is not a mind-reader; they're an interpreter. Hand them a vague hope, and they'll fill the silence with what they'd like to make.

Why most briefs fail

The briefs that go wrong almost always describe deliverables instead of decisions. "We need a modern, premium logo." That feels like a brief. It isn't. "Modern" means seven different things to seven designers; "premium" could mean a patisserie or a private bank. Adjectives are not direction — they're a vibe, and vibes don't survive contact with a deadline.

A real brief gives the designer the reasoning they'd otherwise have to invent.

The six questions a good brief answers

1. What's changing in the business? A rebrand usually means the vision shifted — new market, new line, outgrown story. If nothing's changing, you may need a refresh, not a rebrand.

2. Who exactly is the buyer, and what must they feel? Not "everyone 25–45." The specific person, and what they must understand within five seconds of seeing you.

3. What's the evidence something's broken? Reviews that flinch at the experience, carts abandoned, calls where you keep re-explaining yourself. Symptoms give a target instead of a mood.

4. Who are you up against — and what do you refuse to look like? Three competitors, what works for them, and what you will not be. Knowing what to avoid is half of positioning.

5. What are the hard constraints? Budget, deadline, and the untouchables — the name, a legacy mark, a colour customers already know.

6. What does success look like in 90 days? Something you can actually check, not "people love it."

The honest part

That template takes twenty minutes to fill in — if you know the answers. Most founders can answer three of the six, and the missing three are exactly where projects die. If you're drawing a blank on "what's the evidence" or "who's the buyer," that's not a failure. That is the work — and it's worth doing before you sign anything, because every lakh you spend downstream inherits the quality of these answers.
