Why your last design project missed — and it probably wasn't the designer

By SankalpThe Creative Audit

If you've paid for design that came back technically right and somehow completely wrong, you're not alone — and you're not a bad client. The failure almost always happens in the same invisible place.

Design projects don't usually fail at the logo or the layout. They fail at the brief — the handoff between what you meant and what the designer understood. A designer is an interpreter, not a mind-reader. Give them a vague hope and they'll fill the gaps with what they'd like to make.

What comes back is technically finished and quietly wrong — and nobody can quite say why.

It's a translation problem, not a talent problem

You can't speak design fluently — nobody taught you — and the designer can't read your mind. Without a shared, specific definition of "better," you're both guessing, and you only discover the mismatch at the end, when it's expensive to fix. Hiring a more talented designer doesn't solve this. A brilliant designer pointed at a fuzzy target just produces a more beautiful version of the wrong thing.

Both sides own it

The founder who briefs in adjectives shares it with the studio that never pushed for the reasoning underneath. The fix isn't a better designer — it's a better-defined problem before the work starts: who the buyer is, what's changing, what success looks like, what you refuse to be. Decisions, not vibes.

What "defined" looks like

When both sides agree, in specific terms, on what the work is trying to achieve before anyone designs anything, the project stops being a guessing game. That alignment — done before a rupee is spent on execution — is the single highest-leverage thing you can do to make design money work.

Producing that alignment, on paper, before the expensive part begins — that's the whole job of the audit.
